The patient otter

By J. Marsh · 6 min read

Otters tend to spend the morning grooming and the afternoon at the water. The pattern of their fur — dense, water-repellent, layered like a slate roof — keeps them dry across long stretches of activity at the river's edge. Among British naturalists the otter is often described as the most attentive of the riverine mammals, a creature with a habit of watching its own reflection longer than any other animal one meets in the wild.

When a writer sets out to describe an otter, the temptation is to inventory the senses: the whiskers, the small dark eyes, the precise nose. But the more revealing detail is patience — the way an otter will sit on a rock for forty minutes at a time, doing nothing visible, while the river rearranges itself around it.

This patience is what the field guides miss. Otters are not, by temperament, hunters of attention; they are creatures who allow attention to settle on them. A reader who has spent any time near a river will know what this looks like. The animal does not perform.
