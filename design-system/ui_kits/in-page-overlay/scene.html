How the long-form essay survived the algorithm

For a decade the case against the long essay seemed open and shut. Attention spans were collapsing. Scrolling habits were calcifying. The kind of patient, paragraphs-deep argument that defined a New Yorker piece or a Sunday Times feature was, the conventional wisdom went, headed for extinction. And yet the long-form essay has not died. It has, against the run of play, quietly come back into season — partly because the algorithm started rewarding dwell time, partly because the readers who never left finally felt visible again, and partly because the form itself adapted faster than its critics gave it credit for.

It happened slowly, the way these things do. A magazine that should have folded went and hired a new editor. A newsletter that had been all takes turned into a place where ideas could breathe for two thousand words at a stretch. The flamingo-pink banners that used to scream for clicks shrank to a polite afterthought. And the readers, the ones who had been told they were extinct, came back — quietly, patiently, in numbers nobody quite expected.

Wikipedia · Cetacea

Whales as cultural creatures

Whales are among a small handful of non-human species in which cultural transmission has been rigorously documented. Songs propagate across populations in patterns that bear little resemblance to genetics; feeding strategies pass from mother to calf in ways that more closely resemble apprenticeship than instinct. Among cetaceans the most striking case is the humpback. A new song variant introduced by one population in the eastern Pacific can, over a span of four to six years, sweep across hundreds of kilometres of ocean and reorganise the repertoire of every adult male in its path.
